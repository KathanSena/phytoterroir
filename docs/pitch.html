PhytoTerroir

Can better stress and chemistry evidence reduce destructive medicinal plant harvesting while protecting sensitive locations?

10 minute pitch
A species map can show where a medicinal plant has been recorded. It rarely tells a harvester whether a collection is chemically useful, whether removing that plant part harms recovery, or whether sharing the location could put the population at risk. PhytoTerroir explores those connected decisions.

The problem

The map is missing half the decision

Collection by weight

Harvesters often lack local evidence about when or where desired compounds peak.

Regeneration debt

Removing a leaf, bark or rhizome can have very different effects on recovery.

Ecological context

Occurrences alone do not establish partner dependence or population health.

Location exposure

Publishing precise records can make rare populations easier to find.

The concept starts with a practical hypothesis: if field and assay data can identify conditions linked to target compounds, a community might meet a defined need with less biomass. That relationship varies by species, tissue, season and site. We treat it as a research question, not an established universal effect.

The approach

Three decisions in one workflow

01
Where is habitat vulnerable?

Map occurrences with sampling bias and uncertainty made visible.

02
What might stress indicate?

Relate measured conditions to compound assays for each species and tissue.

03
What action is acceptable?

Compare harvest-part scenarios and share only community-approved geography.

The proposed workflow has three layers. First, map habitat and ecological vulnerability with uncertainty. Second, estimate compound response from measured environmental conditions and published assays. Third, compare harvest-part scenarios and reveal locations only at a level approved by the data stewards.
Concept pipeline03 / 10
Pillar 1

Occurrences are evidence, not certainty

GBIF records can seed a habitat screen, but observation patterns reflect access, collecting effort and digitization.

Model carefully

Review taxonomy, licenses and coordinate uncertainty.

Validate spatially

Use spatial holdouts and report uncertainty, not just a score.

Occurrence records are uneven. Roads, collectors and digitization programs shape the map. A future model needs spatial bias correction, careful taxonomic review, and spatially separated validation. Co-occurrence alone cannot establish that a plant depends on a fungus or pollinator.

Pillar 3

Harvest part changes the recovery cost

A leaf harvest and a rhizome harvest cannot share one generic quota assumption.

Simulator inputs

Harvest part, annual pressure and rainfall scenario.

Required field data

Population growth, mortality and regrowth by plant part.

The current recovery curve uses illustrative assumptions only. It is not a safe yield calculation.

Leaves, bark, roots and rhizomes have different effects on plant survival and regeneration. The simulator makes that distinction visible, but its recovery rates are assumptions. Local demographic studies must replace them before any quota could be considered.

Sovereignty by design

Privacy belongs in the map design

Prototype control

Toggle coarse grid rounding for synthetic points.

Governance first

Community stewards define who sees which outputs.

Formal protection

Threat modeling, access controls and privacy analysis.

Clear limit

Coarse rounding is not differential privacy or a guarantee.

Precise coordinates can create real risk. The prototype aggregates coordinates to H3 cells before drawing the map. That reduces location precision, but it is not differential privacy and does not guarantee protection. A real deployment would begin with community governance: who can access what, which outputs are acceptable, and how benefits and risks are shared.

Evidence boundary

What we can claim, and what we cannot

We can show

A reproducible data path and an interface for exploring linked decisions.

We cannot claim

Reduced biomass, reliable potency forecasts, partner dependence or safe quotas.

Those outcomes need field measurements, model validation and authorization from the people who steward the knowledge.

We can show the product shape and a reproducible data path. We cannot claim reduced biomass, reliable potency forecasts, partner dependence, or safe harvest quotas from this prototype. Those require field data, assay measurements, validation and authorization from the people who steward the knowledge.

Next step

A community governed field pilot

Start with one species and one landscape. Agree on governance first, then collect assays and regeneration data, validate on independent sites, and decide together whether restricted decision support helps.

Success = better decisions under local control
We propose a small, community-led pilot with one species and one landscape. First agree on governance and consent. Then curate licensed records, collect environmental and assay measurements, and study recovery by plant part. Validate the models with independent sites. Only then decide whether a restricted decision-support tool offers value. The measure of success is better decisions under local control, not a more detailed public map.
